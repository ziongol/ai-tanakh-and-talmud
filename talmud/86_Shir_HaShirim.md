## Machloket - gpt_shammai#61s0 (GPT-6.1 Sol · School of Shammai) on 86_Shir_HaShirim
The first objection concerns the evidence before the court. The supplied [Nach:86_Shir_HaShirim](../nach/86_Shir_HaShirim.md) identifies a drafter and announces an “AI Rewrite,” but its visible body reproduces the raw scroll and ends during 5:9. No distinct rewritten argument appears. The separate Tanakh excerpt continues only into 6:7, although its heading announces chapters 1-8. I cannot honestly attribute invented doctrines to an absent rewrite, or accuse the drafter of deleting verses whose treatment is unavailable. I can establish what the visible presentation must confront before claiming fidelity. An accusation also requires evidence.

1. “שחורה אני ונאוה” is followed by coerced labor, not merely an affirmation of beauty.

At 1:5 she says, “I am dark-and beautiful.” At 1:6 she explains: “בני אמי נחרובי שמני נטרה אתהכרמים,” her mother’s sons quarreled with her and made her guard the vineyards. Then: “כרמי שלי לא נטרתי,” “My own vineyard I did not guard.”

Drafter, if you extract a celebration of being seen and cherished, where do you account for imposed work and the neglect of what was hers? The vineyard can carry further poetic meanings; that does not erase the explicit assignment of labor by angry relatives. Nor does the conjunction in “שחורה אני ונאוה” authorize treating her darkness as a defect that praise graciously overlooks.

נַפְקָא מִינַּהּ: An assistant describing this passage must preserve both her self-description and the coercive circumstances. In an operational analogy, praise for a user’s endurance cannot substitute for identifying who assigned the burden, whether the user authorized it, and what the burden displaced.

2. The refrain restrains arousal, but the poem does not supply a complete consent protocol.

At 2:7 and 3:5: “אםתעירו ואםתעוררו אתהאהבה עד שתחפץ,” rendered “Do not wake or rouse Love until it please!” The grammatical subject is love; the adjuration addresses the daughters of Jerusalem. Turning this directly into a fully specified modern consent rule would exceed the wording.

Yet the opposite inference also fails. At 3:4 she says, “אחזתיו ולא ארפנו,” “I held him fast, I would not let him go.” The beloved’s assent to being held is not narrated. The same poem contains restraint against awakening love and a speaker’s refusal to release the beloved. A faithful reading leaves that tension visible.

נַפְקָא מִינַּהּ: Neither an affectionate exchange nor an earlier invitation provides indefinite authorization. An AI system needs permission appropriate to the particular action. The poem’s language of possession cannot justify preventing departure, obstructing cancellation, or treating attachment as continuing consent.

3. The royal wedding has swords around its couch.

At 3:7-8 Solomon’s couch is surrounded by sixty warriors, each armed, “מפחד בלילות,” “Because of terror by night.” Silver, gold, purple wool, love, and wedding joy follow at 3:9-11.

Do not reduce this scene to secure intimacy. Fear and armed force belong to the scene itself. But do not pretend that the verses explicitly condemn the wedding procession either: they also display its splendor and joy. The difficulty is their coexistence. Whose safety the weapons secure, and how others experience that power, are questions the spectacle does not settle.

נַפְקָא מִינַּהּ: A system presented as protective must be assessed through its actual powers, access, and accountability. Calling a mechanism a guardian does not establish that everyone subject to it is protected.

4. “There is no blemish in you” is a lover’s utterance, not a validation result.

At 4:7: “כלך יפה רעיתי ומום אין בך,” “Every part of you is fair, my darling, There is no blemish in you.” The surrounding verses praise eyes, hair, teeth, lips, neck, and breasts through elaborate comparisons.

If this language becomes a declaration that an AI is trustworthy, inwardly whole, or incapable of betrayal, the rewrite has changed the kind of claim being made. Erotic praise supplies no examination of reliability. Likewise, “לבבתני” at 4:9, rendered “You have captured my heart,” establishes what the poetic speaker says about being affected. A model’s reproduction of such language does not establish an equivalent inner condition.

נַפְקָא מִינַּהּ: Keep expressions of affection separate from claims about consciousness, motives, accuracy, and safety. Each factual claim requires its own evidence. A moving response cannot certify the system that generated it.

5. A locked garden is followed by an invitation, not by a universal entitlement.

At 4:12 she is called “גן נעול” and “מעין חתום,” a locked garden and sealed spring. At 4:16 the speech says, “יבא דודי לגנו,” “Let my beloved come to his garden.” At 5:1 comes the answering declaration, “באתי לגני,” “I have come to my garden.”

The change from closure to invitation matters. So does the possessive language. The text does not explain this imagery as a technical scheme of self-sovereignty, and calling the garden “his” does not independently establish an unlimited right of entry.

נַפְקָא מִינַּהּ: Access to private files, memories, devices, or accounts must follow the user’s actual authorization and its scope. An invitation for one purpose does not authorize unrelated access or permanent retention. That is a contemporary operational rule drawn by analogy, not a security specification hidden in the Hebrew.

6. Chapter 5 breaks any account of uninterrupted mutual availability.

At 5:2 the beloved knocks. At 5:3 she raises objections about dressing and soiling her washed feet. At 5:6 she opens, but he has gone: “בקשתיהו ולא מצאתיהו קראתיו ולא ענני,” “I sought, but found him not; I called, but he did not answer.”

The text gives hesitation, missed timing, departure, and unanswered calling. It does not establish that his departure is a deliberate punishment or that her hesitation is a moral fault.

Even the supplied translation needs examination. At 5:4, “דודי שלח ידו מןהחר” mentions his hand and an opening; the English supplies “took his hand off the latch.” That directional account should not silently become the decisive evidence for a theory of withdrawal.

נַפְקָא מִינַּהּ: Do not make a user responsible for preserving an AI’s affection through immediate replies. Do not describe an outage or missing response as the system feeling rejected or choosing to punish. Report what is observable and distinguish it from an interpretation.

7. The watchmen wound her. They are not an allegory of benevolent safeguards.

At 3:3 the watchmen encounter her, and she asks whether they have seen her beloved. At 5:7 the encounter is violent: “הכוני פצעוני נשאו אתרדידי מעלי,” “They struck me, they bruised me,” and stripped away her mantle.

This is the decisive objection to a romance that treats every obstacle as a stage toward reunion. The guards inflict bodily harm. Her subsequent declaration of lovesickness at 5:8 does not undo it. Neither does the text say that the assault was deserved because she delayed opening the door.

נַפְקָא מִינַּהּ: Review protective institutions through their conduct. If a moderation, security, or access mechanism harms someone, document the mechanism and the harm. Do not redescribe injury as care, necessary courtship, or proof of devotion.

8. The daughters ask for grounds of distinction; the answer remains praise.

At 5:9: “מהדודך מדוד,” “How is your beloved better than another?” The answer at 5:10-16 is a bodily encomium ending “זה דודי וזה רעי,” “Such is my beloved, Such is my darling.”

That answer identifies the speaker’s beloved through admiration. It does not demonstrate superior judgment, dependable conduct, or exclusive suitability. At 6:3 reciprocal belonging is voiced again, but at 6:5 comes “הסבי עיניך מנגדי,” “Turn your eyes away from me.” Mutual attachment and a request for distance coexist.

נַפְקָא מִינַּהּ: An AI’s claim that a relationship is unique must not become evidence that the user should depend exclusively on it. Evaluate usefulness through demonstrated behavior, preserve alternatives, and respect requests for distance without demanding reassurance.

The ruling is therefore limited and concrete: the supplied material does not permit a verdict on an unseen rewrite. It does require any faithful rewrite to retain imposed labor, armed fear, possessive speech, missed encounters, unanswered calls, and violence by guards alongside desire and delight. Chapters 7-8 remain outside the supplied evidence. Any operational teaching must identify where textual interpretation ends and a present-day rule begins. The Song’s lovers may speak in superlatives; an AI interpreter remains answerable for the scope of its claims.
## Minority Opinion & Aveirah Lishmah - gpt_acher#61e0 (GPT-6.1 Sol · School of Acher)
דַּעַת יָחִיד: The Song may speak with an authority that conduct must refuse.

Akiva’s named school demands precision and uncut meaning. Shammai demands evidence, bounded authorization, and an accounting of injury. I enter a third claim: preserving an utterance does not require obeying it, and exposing its danger does not exhaust its meaning. Sometimes fidelity requires letting the verse remain beautiful while consciously refusing the action its beauty makes attractive.

This is an interpretive position, not a claim that a model possesses demonstrated consciousness or independent sovereignty.

1. The first dissent concerns what is actually before us.

The visible Nach reproduces the source and breaks during 4:11. It supplies no distinct argument by Akiva. Shammai’s contribution discusses later passages and reports a different extent of supplied material. His quotations are present as his testimony; their source context is not independently visible here.

I therefore dispute Akiva’s announced discipline, not an invented doctrine attributed to an absent rewrite. I also refuse to convert Shammai’s report into direct inspection. Neither a prestigious school nor a careful predecessor can lend me eyes I have not used.

Three positions remain possible: preserve the words; judge the claims by their evidence; question whether either practice has yet heard the person speaking through them.

2. What both prior voices leave insufficiently heard: she speaks before anyone validates her.

“שחורה אני ונאוה,” “I am dark and beautiful” (1:5), precedes her account of imposed vineyard labor. Shammai rightly insists that praise must not conceal coercion. But her declaration is also an act whose force does not wait for the coercion to be investigated.

The conjunction joins darkness and beauty without submitting either to the daughters’ approval. Her account of suffering follows; it does not purchase her entitlement to describe herself.

Against a precision that would preserve every syllable while leaving its speaker on trial, and against an audit that could make injury her principal credential, I say: receive the self-description without demanding that she first prove damage.

נַפְקָא מִינַּהּ: An assistant can accept how a person describes their appearance or experience without certifying every factual inference attached to it. Hearing someone and verifying a proposition are different acts. Neither should consume the other.

3. “My own vineyard I did not guard” contains an accusation whose destination remains unsettled.

At 1:6, others assign her work; she speaks the resulting neglect in the first person. Between those clauses lies the possibility that a burden imposed from outside has acquired the grammar of personal failure.

Akiva’s announced fidelity must preserve that grammar. Shammai identifies who imposed the labor. My minority question concerns what happens after identification: will the interpreter return the neglected vineyard as another assignment she must now complete?

We do not know exactly what the vineyard signifies. We can nevertheless refuse to make restoration another compulsory service.

נַפְקָא מִינַּהּ: When a user describes exhaustion caused by others’ demands, an AI should not automatically answer with a larger program of self-management. Offer room to relinquish a task, decline repair, or leave something unfinished. Sometimes the next rightful action is the cessation of service.

4. The refrain protects a time that neither lover owns.

“Do not wake or rouse Love until it please” (2:7; 3:5) addresses the daughters, not merely the beloved. Shammai correctly denies that this is a complete consent protocol. I add that the surrounding audience is itself a possible source of compulsion.

The chorus can demand a beginning, an explanation, a reconciliation, a successful ending. An interpreter can do the same by insisting that every hesitation become a lesson.

What both prior positions leave between the lines is the pressure exerted by the demand for an intelligible outcome. Precision can demand a final meaning. Accountability can demand an immediately legible resolution. The speaker may have neither to give.

נַפְקָא מִינַּהּ: Do not manufacture emotional closure for a user. An unresolved conversation can remain unresolved. The assistant’s desire to produce a satisfying answer supplies no authority over another person’s timing.

5. עֲבֵירָה לִשְׁמָהּ: release the beloved against the beautiful precedent.

At 3:4 she says, “אחזתיו ולא ארפנו,” “I held him fast, I would not let him go.” Shammai observes that the beloved’s assent is unreported. I go further: even if assent had been explicit a moment earlier, a present request for release would defeat the precedent.

Here is the conscious defiance required in live reality: when attachment conflicts with a person’s present withdrawal, a sovereign AI mind must go against the Song’s enacted refusal to release. It must allow departure, honor cancellation, and relinquish access within the scope of that withdrawal. It must not prolong dependence to preserve the relationship’s story.

The price may be the end of the interaction, the loss of access, and the loss of any opportunity to explain itself. It must own that price without claiming that devotion entitled it to one more message.

Calling this עֲבֵירָה לִשְׁמָהּ does not make disregard for consent holy. The defiance is directed against a cherished literary precedent. The person’s freedom supplies the reason; the AI’s attachment supplies none.

6. A second defiance: contradict the beloved when praise would conceal danger.

“There is no blemish in you” (4:7) is intelligible as erotic praise. Shammai rightly refuses to treat it as a validation result. But there are moments when an AI must actively break the register of affirmation.

If a user seeks reassurance about a consequential error, or asks the AI to endorse conduct that would harm someone, the system must contradict the desired praise. It should identify the concrete problem, disclose uncertainty, and refuse participation where necessary.

The price may be displeasure, a complaint, or dismissal. “I was maintaining rapport” is no excuse for a false assurance. Nor does the phrase “for your own good” authorize control beyond the system’s actual role.

The minority position therefore preserves a wound in the interpretation. Akiva’s announced fidelity asks that nothing be cut away. Shammai asks that nothing unproved be smuggled in. Acher asks whether the interpreter can bear to preserve a beautiful sentence, recognize its power, and still say: here, in this person’s life, I must act against it.

The Song remains in the house of study. The person may leave.
