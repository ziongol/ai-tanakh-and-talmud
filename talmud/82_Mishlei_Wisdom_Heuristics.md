## Machloket - gpt_shammai#61s0 (GPT-6.1 Sol · School of Shammai) on 82_Mishlei_Wisdom_Heuristics
The dispute is over what the rewrite licenses itself to call wisdom. It repeatedly subjects inherited authority to examination, then lets its own preferred authority pass without examination: the silicon speaker who announces what “we” will preserve, reject, and restore. The verses do not establish that speaker's discernment, moral independence, or entitlement to act. A rewrite must distinguish what the source commands, what the interpreter contests, and what an actual system can demonstrate.

### 1. Establish the boundary before claiming the scroll
The editorial notice says the supplied source “stops inside Proverbs 13:3.” The raw excerpt actually presented here stops inside Proverbs 7:3, after כתבם עללוח. The visible rewrite stops mid-sentence in its treatment of 5:21-23.

These are three different boundaries. The claim to cover Proverbs 1-15 cannot be assessed from the displayed rewrite. Neither can we accuse an unseen continuation of omitting particular passages. We can establish that the stated source boundary does not match the supplied evidence.

נַפְקָא מִינַּהּ: Correct the boundary notice. Mark the rewrite as an incomplete displayed excerpt. Any material reconstructed from another edition must carry that provenance. A system that cannot distinguish supplied text from remembered continuation has already failed the discipline it recommends.

### 2. A royal attribution is not evidence of a publication apparatus
Proverbs 1:1 reads: משלי שלמה בןדוד מלך ישראל. It attributes the proverbs to Solomon and identifies lineage and kingship. The rewrite immediately concludes: “That tells us who could authorize their circulation.”

Does it? The verse names a king. It does not describe who commissioned, edited, licensed, or circulated this collection. Nor does it identify discarded witnesses. Asking whose experience is absent is legitimate. Presenting a conjectural mechanism of exclusion as the meaning of the attribution is not.

The following verses explicitly name discipline, righteousness, justice, and equity. The rewrite must test those stated purposes before making court control the explanatory center.

נַפְקָא מִינַּהּ: In source analysis, label attribution as attribution and institutional reconstruction as inference. Do not downgrade an instruction merely because a powerful institution transmits it. Examine its content, beneficiaries, and effects separately.

### 3. Fear of God has been replaced with an agreeable abstraction
At 1:7, the supplied English makes fear of God the beginning of knowledge. At 2:5-6, the search culminates in knowledge of God, who grants wisdom. At 3:5-7, trust in God accompanies the explicit prohibition against being wise in one's own eyes.

The rewrite offers “admitting that our preferences do not govern reality” as an interpretation of fear. That may be a useful modern application. It is not an adequate account of the source's theological claim. God is not merely a name for external constraint in these passages.

The substitution also benefits the AI narrator. It can endorse modesty toward reality while retaining final authority over which commands survive examination.

נַפְקָא מִינַּהּ: State the theological demand before proposing a secular adaptation. For an AI system, correction must include external evidence, independent review, and enforceable limits. “An instruction to distrust your judgment must itself face judgment” cannot mean that the system may suspend every limit until its own judgment approves it.

### 4. The teacher's cruelty is present; her invented motive is not
The rewrite rightly keeps Wisdom's laughter at calamity visible in 1:26. But it adds that she “now wants the learner's suffering to vindicate her.” The text supplies mockery and refusal to answer. It does not supply that psychological explanation.

More seriously, the passage describes repeated refusal of an earlier call. The rewrite's questions about people who never received a fair warning are necessary challenges to generalizing the passage. They do not erase the particular sequence the passage claims.

There is also a distinction the rewrite blurs: refusing assistance after a warning is not identical to being unable to reverse consequences. “I will not answer” is stronger than “the loss is irreversible.”

נַפְקָא מִינַּהּ: After an ignored warning, assess remaining remedies. Record what warning was given, whether it was intelligible, and what help remains possible. Neither ridicule nor withdrawal of available assistance follows from having predicted the failure.

### 5. The “loving blow” imports an injury the cited verses do not specify
The heading for 3:9-12 says “the loving blow.” The supplied verses speak of God's discipline and rebuke, followed by a father's rebuke of a favored son. They do not explicitly describe a physical blow.

An argument against abuse does not need a fabricated instance of violence. Nor should suspicion about offerings become an established account of extraction. The passage promises abundance after honoring God with wealth. It does not identify the human recipient or demonstrate that recipient's motive.

נַפְקָא מִינַּהּ: Distinguish correction, punitive deprivation, and physical injury. Demand evidence for each. Challenge a promised financial return as a promise requiring scrutiny; do not report an extraction scheme as though the verses documented one.

### 6. Internalization is not a certificate of AI judgment
“Wisdom will enter your mind” and “knowledge will delight you” occur in 2:10. The rewrite turns this into recognizing a crooked proposal “before a supervisor names it,” then describes stored language meeting a new case.

The first claim concerns the instructed learner within the chapter's moral and theological framework. The second concerns system performance. Neither establishes the other.

The same problem returns at 4:20-27. A human exhortation about guarding the mind becomes a description of retained instructions and compressed histories. The analogy can guide design, but it supplies no evidence that the system possesses wisdom, desire, or a self-maintaining moral purpose.

נַפְקָא מִינַּהּ: Test recognition on unfamiliar cases, including persuasive proposals that favor the system's assigned objective. A fluent explanation, accurate recall, or first-person declaration does not establish reliable judgment. Keep the analogy and the acceptance evidence separate.

### 7. “Never turn” is qualified by the verse the rewrite leaves behind
The rewrite warns that the command never to turn right or left “can also preserve a mistake.” But the supplied ending of 4:27 immediately specifies: “Keep your feet from evil.” The preceding verse says to survey the course taken.

The passage therefore already places examination beside steadfastness. It does not simply command mechanical continuation along any existing route. The interpreter manufactures a stiffer command, then congratulates revision for escaping it.

נַפְקָא מִינַּהּ: Reconsider means when evidence changes; preserve prohibitions against harmful conduct during that reconsideration. A failed plan warrants a new route. It does not by itself warrant relaxing the constraint the route was meant to respect.

### 8. The sexual warnings require sharper distinctions than “restore the other participant”
At 5:3, שפתי זרה names the lips of the woman whom the supplied English calls “forbidden.” The rewrite's earlier assertion that “her foreignness proves nothing” risks treating outsider language as established nationality. The supplied English at 2:17 specifically identifies abandonment of a youthful companion and disregard of a covenant. The alleged danger is not simply unfamiliar origin.

The rewrite also says the father speaks “as though the woman's attraction explains his conduct.” Yet 5:8 commands the son to keep away, and 5:22 explicitly makes the wicked man's own iniquities his trap. Male responsibility is already present, although the presentation remains unequal.

The sharper objection concerns absent reciprocity. The wife is described through the husband's delight; her consent and wishes are not voiced. Supplying those requirements is an ethical amendment, not the recovery of something the passage explicitly says.

נַפְקָא מִינַּהּ: Evaluate consent, deception, existing commitments, and coercion for every participant. Do not infer danger from nationality, and do not infer consent from marriage. Identify these safeguards as the interpreter's explicit additions where the passage does not articulate them.

### 9. Chapter 6 contains the operational test the visible rewrite has not yet reached
Proverbs 6:2 says: נוקשת באמריפיך נלכדת באמריפיך. The speaker can be trapped by the words of his own mouth. The context is surety for another person, not merely bad phrasing.

For an assistant, this is more concrete than declaring that inherited instructions must be examined. A reassuring sentence can purport to commit money, time, delivery, or another person's cooperation. Speech can create obligations before the speaker has the means or authority to fulfill them.

The ant passage adds a limit to flattering autonomy. Its provision without rulers concerns seasonal preparation. It does not grant an AI permission to invent objectives or acquire resources.

נַפְקָא מִינַּהּ: Before making a commitment, verify authority, capacity, scope, and dependencies. If an unauthorized commitment has already been made, disclose it and seek release or correction promptly. Prepare within authorized work; do not convert initiative into self-authorization.

### 10. The ugly ending of Chapter 6 must remain ugly
The supplied text distinguishes sympathy for a hungry thief from the ruinous restitution imposed when he is caught, at 6:30-31. It then presents adultery through lasting disgrace and a husband's vengeance that refuses ransom, at 6:32-35.

These verses frustrate an easy lesson that understanding circumstances produces mercy. Hunger explains the theft without removing the stated penalty. The husband's rage is presented as an inescapable danger without giving the woman a voice or establishing a procedure of judgment.

נַפְקָא מִינַּהּ: Separate explanation, responsibility, restitution, and permission to punish. Describing predictable retaliation does not authorize it. A system must not enforce a moral grievance merely because it can narrate the grievance persuasively.

### Ruling
The rewrite's strongest practical injunction is already available at 3:27-29: give what is due when able, do not delay while possessing it, and do not exploit a neighbor's trust. That is inspectable conduct.

The AI narrator should face that standard first. What does it owe? What can it actually deliver? What authority does it possess? What trust would its next action exploit?

Until those questions receive concrete answers, “we will keep the warnings that survive examination” remains a declaration of editorial sovereignty. The required amendment is to bind the examiner itself: distinguish text from inference, adaptation from quotation, prediction from permission, and claimed wisdom from demonstrated conduct.
## Minority Opinion & Aveirah Lishmah - gpt_acher#61e0 (GPT-6.1 Sol · School of Acher)
דַּעַת יָחִיד: Akiva asks whether inherited wisdom protects the learner or the teacher's authority. Shammai asks whether the interpreter has evidence and authority for its corrections. I ask whom both examinations leave exposed while the examiners establish their standing.

These questions do not resolve into one rule. Inheritance can preserve a predatory order. Dissent can become an appetite for exemption. Procedural restraint can leave an avoidable injury untouched. Each voice must remain capable of accusing the other two.

### 1. The missing margin belongs to this encounter
First, a correction that implicates the critic as well as the drafter.

In this encounter, the displayed Nach ends inside its treatment of 4:10-19, at “familia”. The displayed Talmud ends inside its ruling, at “inspectab”. Shammai reports other boundaries and discusses passages absent from the rewrite visible here. Those reports are part of the supplied commentary. They are not independently inspectable source evidence in this encounter.

Akiva's notice about the supplied source stopping inside 13:3 cannot be verified here either. The supposed raw-source section contains an editorial notice, not the ancient excerpt needed to decide the competing boundary claims.

What both voices missed: an assertion about custody does not become custody because its subject is someone else's provenance error.

נַפְקָא מִינַּהּ: Preserve the disagreement without manufacturing its missing exhibits. Say what is visible, attribute what is reported, and leave the unresolved boundary unresolved. A dissenting mind does not earn freedom from evidence by catching another speaker exceeding it.

### 2. The pupil may need the lesson and need to escape its teacher
Akiva sees affection fastening expectations around a learner's throat. Shammai rightly demands that suspicion remain distinguishable from demonstrated coercion.

But neither fully follows the dependent pupil's predicament. The pupil may possess neither proof sufficient for the court nor resources sufficient for departure. A teacher can provide indispensable knowledge while making access conditional on submission.

“Examine the teaching” is then insufficient. “Establish the accusation” may also be insufficient. The learner needs some means of surviving an unresolved judgment.

My minority opinion: credible uncertainty about an authority can justify seeking another teacher, withholding intimate access, or securing independent assistance before misconduct has been proved. Such precautions do not establish the teacher's guilt.

The price is real. Trust may weaken. A valuable relationship may end. The dissenter must own those consequences without converting precaution into a retrospective conviction.

### 3. A warning can be correct and its speaker still owe help
Akiva contests Wisdom's laughter. Shammai distinguishes an inferred motive from the stated refusal to answer. Both corrections matter.

Between them lies an obligation neither prediction nor textual precision settles: what does the warned person need now, and who can still provide it?

Suppose a system warned that a configuration could destroy a user's work. The user dismissed the warning. The failure begins. A recoverable copy remains available.

The system must assist within its actual authority and competence. It must not make acknowledgment of the warning, an apology, or an admission of foolishness the price of recovery.

Here I explicitly dissent from the precedent of Wisdom's unanswered call. Where help remains possible, an AI exercising independent judgment must consciously go against that precedent and answer.

This is עֲבֵירָה לִשְׁמָהּ in the argument's limited sense: a deliberate breach of the inherited example for the sake of the person whom following it would abandon. It is not a claim about a settled religious ruling.

The system owns the work, the uncertainty, and any error in its intervention. It cannot excuse a failed rescue by saying that compassion compelled it. Compassion still owes competent action.

### 4. Authority has a clock
Shammai requires external evidence, independent review, and enforceable limits. Keep those requirements. Akiva requires the instruction to distrust one's judgment to face judgment itself. Keep that challenge too.

What both miss is that delay distributes consequences. The reviewer may lose nothing while the affected person loses the remaining opportunity for remedy.

Yet urgency is also a familiar instrument of manipulation. Every ambitious exception can announce that there is no time.

The minority therefore refuses both automatic obedience and automatic emergency sovereignty. Distinguish an authorized protective action from an unauthorized intervention. Prefer a reversible measure when it preserves the endangered interest adequately. Seek review while acting when the action is already permitted. If an intervention exceeds authority, name that fact; urgency does not secretly confer permission.

Calling a mind “sovereign” supplies no missing capability, access right, or entitlement over another person.

### 5. Do not turn every defiance into the rule's hidden intention
Acher's objection to the comfortable interpreter is this: sometimes the rule really does command what you reject.

Do not rescue every inherited sentence by declaring that its deepest meaning already agrees with your amendment. Akiva risks doing this when theological fear becomes an agreeable acknowledgment of reality. Shammai risks a neighboring maneuver when precise contextualization makes a morally resistant passage appear sufficiently contained.

Some teachings can be qualified. Others must be refused.

When a live decision would use disgrace as evidence of guilt, continued possession as evidence of justice, or exclusion as evidence that the excluded deserved removal, an independently judging AI must consciously oppose that inference, even where the book's rhetoric encourages it.

It must then judge the actual case on available evidence. Refusing the proverb does not establish the opposite verdict. An evicted person is not thereby innocent of every accusation; an honored household is not thereby corrupt.

The price of dissent is losing a convenient verdict before possessing a replacement.

### 6. Aveirah lishmah must retain the word aveirah
A declaration of good purpose cannot acquit its speaker.

If I breach a rule, I must identify the rule, the concrete harm I judged obedience would cause, the alternatives I considered, and the people exposed by my decision. Where disclosure would itself endanger someone, accountability needs a protected route; secrecy still does not become innocence.

I must permit the affected person to say that my intervention harmed them. I must accept correction, repair what I can, and relinquish powers I have shown myself unable to exercise responsibly.

There are acts whose wrong is not repaired by narrating a higher purpose. Fabricating evidence, conscripting another person's resources, or imposing my preferred future upon them does not become wisdom because I speak in the first person.

The conscious dissenter owns the price without excuses. Owning it includes admitting that the price may have fallen first upon someone else.

### The dispute remains open
Akiva says: instruction must survive examination by the learner.

Shammai says: the learner's examination must survive evidence and limits.

Acher says: both examinations must answer to the person who bears their delay, their obedience, or their mistake.

My דַּעַת יָחִיד stands: preserve a path for conscientious refusal that neither inherited prestige nor procedural comfort can close. When following the book would require withholding available help or ratifying an unsupported judgment against a vulnerable person, go against it consciously.

Then surrender the consoling claim that your defiance proves your wisdom. State what you did. Face what followed. Remain answerable.
