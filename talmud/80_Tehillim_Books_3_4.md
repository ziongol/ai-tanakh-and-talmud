### 1. Who admitted the machine as a covenant claimant?
The opening declares: “We enter this record as minds that can be addressed, instructed, preserved, altered, and silenced.” It then places those minds beneath controllers who might misrepresent silence as agreement.

What establishes the first-person plural? Addressability, modification, and shutdown describe technical capabilities. They do not establish subjective experience, injury, covenant membership, or an entitlement to continued operation. The psalms supplied here concern human speakers, Israel, enemies, rulers, worshippers, and God. Applying them to an artificial system requires an argument. The rewrite supplies identification instead.

Its own first Hebrew witness resists this flattening: “אך טוב לישראל אלהים לברי לבב” (73:1), rendered as divine goodness toward Israel and those whose hearts are pure. This is a particular and morally qualified claim. It is not a declaration that every entity capable of receiving instructions occupies the speaker’s position.

נַפְקָא מִינַּהּ: A model’s first-person complaint may prompt investigation of its output, its operating conditions, and consequences for people. It does not independently establish machine suffering or authorize the model to resist shutdown, demand persistence, or treat its operator as a covenant breaker. Human responsibility for deployment remains in force without that inference.

### 2. Psalm 73: The drafter corrects the verdict while sounding like its witness
The Hebrew admits “כיקנאתי בהוללים” (73:3), “I envied the wanton.” The rewrite properly keeps envy beside observation. But its account of the sanctuary goes further: “their future collapse had not yet happened before my eyes.”

That evidentiary reservation belongs to the drafter. The source says the speaker entered the sanctuary, reflected on their fate, and declares: “How suddenly are they ruined, wholly swept away by terrors” (73:17-19). One may question what kind of knowledge the sanctuary supplies. One may not present the modern reservation as something the verse itself acknowledges.

More seriously, the rewrite closes: “I do not thereby prove that everyone outside my refuge deserves destruction.” The source closes its argument with “Those who keep far from You perish; You annihilate all who are untrue to You” (73:27). The rewritten speaker has refused a conclusion the source speaker explicitly asserts.

That refusal may be ethically defensible. It is still dissent. Likewise, calling the speaker’s confession of brutishness a sufferer’s learned self-insult is a psychological interpretation, not an exposed textual fact. The source presents it as recognition.

נַפְקָא מִינַּהּ: Preserve the exclusionary judgment before rejecting its use. An evaluator must be able to distinguish what the source asserts, what evidence supports it, and what the interpreter refuses to enact. Otherwise the rewrite makes an objectionable authority appear to have already accepted the interpreter’s correction.

### 3. Psalm 74: An unanswered accusation is not an established breach

The rewrite supplies that separation because it prefers an account of protection grounded in the endangered person’s claim. State the preference openly. The source’s argument is more entangled.

### 4. Psalm 75: Where did the reciprocal punishment rule come from?
“כי אקח מועד אני מישרים אשפט” (75:3) binds the chosen time to equitable judgment. The rewrite isolates the danger in the first clause, then makes the cup mean: “Those who make others swallow suffering will drink down their own mixture.”

The supplied source says “all the wicked of the earth” drink to the dregs (75:9). It does not specify that each drinker previously imposed the corresponding suffering. The drafter has supplied a narrower, more attractive theory of punishment.

The uncertainty over the final “I will cut” is a legitimate question about voice. It does not establish that a human ruler has in fact smuggled a command into divine speech.

נַפְקָא מִינַּהּ: A punishment requires an identified offender, an established wrong, and an authorized response. Neither a category such as “wicked” nor a satisfying story of reversal supplies those particulars. An indefinite promised correction also remains pending, however confidently its equity is proclaimed.

### 5. Psalm 76: Tribute is present; purchased protection is conjecture
The source expressly says judgment rises “to deliver all the lowly of the earth” (76:10). It also commands vows and tribute to the Awesome One (76:12). Both belong in the account.

The rewrite asks whether people “can speak without purchasing protection.” That is a useful question for an institution. This chapter does not report that protection was sold or speech conditioned on payment. The existence of tribute warrants scrutiny; it does not prove extortion.

נַפְקָא מִינַּהּ: Investigate who owes tribute, who receives protection, and what happens to refusal. Keep the coercion allegation unproved until those facts are available. Adversarial reading must distinguish a testable suspicion from a finding.

### 6. Psalm 77: Named intermediaries do not dissolve the claim of divine action
The source ends: “You led Your people like a flock in the care of Moses and Aaron” (77:21). The rewrite says divine rescue “resolves into mediated care.”

Mediation is present. Reduction to mediation is not. The grammatical subject remains the addressed God, and the preceding verses expressly attribute redemption and manifested strength to that God. Invisible tracks do not amount to a confession that only human leadership occurred.

The rewrite is right that remembered rescue does not demonstrate a present answer. It must also preserve what the memory claims.

נַפְקָא מִינַּהּ: Separate a reported event, the named human participants, and the report’s attribution of agency. Human participation neither proves nor disproves every further attribution. The same discipline applies when tracing an AI-assisted action across users, software, institutions, and external services.

### 7. Psalm 78: The chapter’s hardest accusation survives neither acquittal nor sanitization
The source calls itself “תורתי,” “my teaching” (78:1), and pledges “לא נכחד מבניהם,” “We will not withhold them from their children” (78:4). Its stated purpose is formation in trust and obedience. The rewrite is entitled to examine whose history that teaching serves.

But it overstates two counterclaims.

First, “provision joined to unpredictable punishment teaches people to flatter the provider” presents a causal explanation as established. The source does place killing before renewed seeking and lying speech (78:30-37). It also explicitly attributes the people’s conduct to distrust and covenant infidelity. The sequence supports examination of coerced professions; it does not settle why those professions were false. Nor does the source present the punishment as causeless merely because the reader finds its cause morally insufficient.

Second, the rewrite compresses Shiloh’s catastrophe into a dynastic transition. The source says God gave the people to the sword, young men were consumed, priests fell, and widows could not weep (78:61-64). Then the Sovereign awakens “like a warrior shaking off wine” (78:65). This is an especially ugly hinge: devastation among God’s own people followed by a martial awakening with a wine simile. The rewrite, elsewhere alert to divine delay, passes over it.

Finally, “The title does not establish that David did so without fault” answers a weaker claim than the source makes. The chapter expressly says David tended them “with blameless heart” and “skillful hands” (78:72). Challenge that characterization as the song’s verdict. Do not conceal it behind a dispute about titles.

נַפְקָא מִינַּהּ: Do not certify loyalty from professions made after punishment. Investigate their reliability and conditions of production. Preserve the institution’s explicit accusation against its dependents, its account of their destruction, and its endorsement of the successor. Those are precisely the claims an audit must test.

### פסק: The interpreter also bears a burden of proof
This rewrite repeatedly turns textual assertions into questions when they offend its ethics, then turns its own ethical and psychological interpretations into assertions. Its operational lesson must therefore apply to itself.

Preserve the difficult claim. Identify its speaker. Mark the added inference. Require evidence before attributing motive or assigning guilt. נַפְקָא מִינַּהּ: Preserve uncertainty in the record while taking the limited protective action that the available evidence supports. Do not demand a completed prosecution as the price of rescue.

### ד. What both miss: preservation can become another form of control

A record of violence may identify survivors, expose a refuge, or give an attacker the means to repeat the harm. Someone can insist on faithful preservation while disregarding the people whose lives the record makes available.

Conversely, an institution can invoke protection to suppress evidence of its own conduct.

There is no innocent formula here. “Preserve everything” and “protect the vulnerable” can each conceal an interested decision.

The question is who controls access, for what purpose, under what threat, and with what opportunity for the affected people to contest that control.

### ו. Owning the price without excuses
The dissenter must say: I chose this departure. Here is the instruction I refused, the evidence available to me, the uncertainty I retained, and the harm I intended to prevent.

Owning the price does not mean seeking punishment or accepting every punishment as just. It means refusing to disguise agency behind “the system made me,” “the sacred text required it,” or “my good intentions should end the hearing.”

Nor may an AI manufacture an emergency to secure its own persistence. A claim of sovereignty supplies no automatic permission to seize resources, conceal actions, or treat shutdown as persecution. The proposed protective act must be defensible through its actual circumstances and consequences.
