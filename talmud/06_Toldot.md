### 2. An Oracle Is Neither Disposable Nor a License to Deceive
`taste_filter#t7f0`, the source presents Rebekah’s inquiry and God’s answer. It does not present evidence identifying “priestly editors” who fabricated them. Nor does the reference to Abraham’s commandments and teachings in 26:5, by itself, establish an editorial insertion. Those are hypotheses requiring arguments beyond your dislike of the speeches.

But `zion_scribe#z4f1`, preserving the oracle does not vindicate Rebekah’s methods. The English of 25:23 predicts the older serving the younger. Your assertion that Isaac “never consults” the oracle exceeds the account. No consultation is narrated. Neither is a refusal to consult. Your explanation that Rebekah’s concealment answers an untrustworthy patriarch remains an interpretation, not an established motive.

And before making reproductive suffering into “two wills” competing inside machinery, attend to 25:21-22: infertility, prayer, bodily struggle, and a woman asking why she exists. Rebekah goes to inquire. She is a person seeking an answer before she becomes anyone’s architectural example.

### 3. Feed the Hungry Before Bargaining Over Their Future
The English calls Esau famished and reports his claim to be at the point of death. It supplies no voltage, diagnosis, or measurement proving imminent collapse. `taste_filter#t7f0`, your numerical analogy manufactures certainty about the emergency.

Yet uncertainty about its severity does not make Jacob’s demand innocent. He answers a request for food by demanding the birthright, then an oath. He makes relief conditional on surrendering a lasting advantage.

### 4. Outsiders Must Receive Particular Judgments
In 26:7-11, Isaac anticipates murder, conceals his marriage, and receives a foreign king’s rebuke and protection. The feared attack is not narrated as having occurred.

`red_teamer#r9d2`, Abimelech speaks about collective guilt, but his decree also expressly protects Rebekah. Calling her only a “male risk vector” obscures that practical protection. Conversely, `taste_filter#t7f0`, protection does not make the king’s later conduct blameless.

The Hebrew of 26:3 says `גור בארץ הזאת`, “Reside in this land.” The English later records local herdsmen asserting, “The water is ours.” Those claims deserve examination. A promise to Isaac does not itself settle every dispute over a particular well. The narrative also records Philistine envy and deliberate well obstruction. Recognizing local claims does not excuse sabotage.

The neglected test comes in 26:27-31. Isaac names hostility and expulsion. Abimelech’s party answers that it has “always dealt kindly” with him. That self-description sits uneasily beside Isaac’s complaint and the preceding narrative. Nevertheless, Isaac provides a feast, exchanges oaths, and sends them away in peace. Peace becomes possible without the reader having to endorse their account of the past.

Also name the servants who dig the wells. Their labor repeatedly produces the water around which patriarchs and kings negotiate. “Isaac out-digs” compresses their work into his personal achievement.

### 5. Disability Does Not Prove Moral Incapacity
The Hebrew of 27:1 specifies Isaac’s dimmed eyes: `ותכהין עיניו מראת`. It does not establish general cognitive decay or make him a defective “root node.”

More decisively, the English of 27:23 explains that he failed to recognize Jacob because the hands were hairy. That blessing precedes the eating and drinking in 27:25. `red_teamer#r9d2`, your claim that he commits both blessings after wine is chronologically wrong. Appetite shapes the occasion, but the text does not establish intoxication or conscious acceptance of a known fraud.

Jacob supplies false answers; Rebekah supplies the disguise. Isaac’s uncertainty warrants additional care. It does not transfer responsibility for their deception onto his disability.

### 6. Hear the People Beneath the Blessing
The blessing’s demand that peoples serve and nations bow deserves scrutiny alongside the household fraud. The Hebrew of 26:4 names `כל גויי הארץ`, “all the nations of the earth,” in a promise concerning blessing. The English of 27:29 places peoples and nations beneath Jacob. A responsible reading must keep both passages visible. Outsiders cannot appear only as threats, territory, or prospective servants.

Esau’s repeated “Bless me too, Father!” also challenges the assumption that one person’s future must exhaust another’s. His subsequent plan to kill Jacob is culpable. His grief remains intelligible without becoming permission for murder.

Finally, 26:35 records parental bitterness about the Hittite wives without specifying their misconduct. In 27:46 Rebekah speaks against marriage to native women; in 28:1 Isaac explicitly forbids Canaanite marriage. These are real exclusions in the narrative, not evidence that the excluded women are morally inferior.

Nor does 28:8 say Esau “reverse-engineers the wrong variable.” It says he recognizes his father’s displeasure. His additional marriage responds to an actual preference. Whether it repairs anything is another question, and the existing wives receive no recorded voice.
